Why Morton's Neuroma Pain Persists: PRP Science and Your Medical Next Steps

Morton's neuroma is a painful condition involving thickening of the tissue around an interdigital nerve, most often between the third and fourth toes. While many treatments ease symptoms, some people continue to experience burning, shooting pain or numbness. Understanding why pain persists—and how platelet-rich plasma (PRP) might help—can empower you to make informed choices.

Why Pain Persists in Morton's Neuroma

Misdiagnosis or Overlapping Conditions• Morton's neuroma can mimic or coexist with metatarsalgia, stress fractures, arthritis or tarsal tunnel syndrome.• If the true source of pain isn't identified, treatments may fail to provide lasting relief.

Biomechanical Factors• High arches, flat feet or tight calf muscles alter forefoot pressure, repeatedly compressing the nerve.• Inadequate footwear or lack of supportive insoles can perpetuate nerve irritation.

Chronic Inflammation and Fibrosis• Ongoing compression triggers local inflammation.• Over time, perineural fibrosis (scar-like tissue) forms, further pinching the nerve.

Central Sensitization• Prolonged pain may "rewire" how your central nervous system processes signals, amplifying discomfort even after the original injury heals.• This neuropathic component can be harder to treat with standard anti‐inflammatories.

How PRP for Morton's Neuroma Works

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an emerging treatment that uses your own blood's growth factors to promote healing. Here's the science:

• Preparation – A small blood sample is spun in a centrifuge to concentrate platelets. – The resulting PRP contains two- to five-times more growth factors than normal blood.

• Mechanisms of Action – Anti-inflammatory: PRP reduces levels of inflammatory cytokines around the nerve. – Tissue Remodeling: Growth factors like PDGF and TGF-β encourage healthy collagen formation, potentially softening fibrotic bands. – Neuroprotection: Some lab studies suggest PRP may support nerve cell survival and reduce pain‐signal transmission.

• Procedure Details – Under ultrasound guidance, a clinician injects PRP precisely around the affected nerve. – A typical course involves 1–3 injections spaced 2–4 weeks apart. – Most patients experience minimal discomfort; common side effects include mild injection‐site soreness or bruising.

• Current Evidence – Small case series and pilot studies show pain relief lasting 6–12 months in many patients. – High-quality randomized controlled trials are still limited—but early results are promising, especially for those who haven't responded to steroids.

Optimize Conservative Measures• Footwear: switch to wide-toe-box, low-heeled shoes with cushioning.• Orthotics: custom arch supports can redistribute pressure away from the metatarsal heads.• Stretching & Strengthening: calf and foot-intrinsic muscle exercises may improve biomechanics.• Activity Modification: limit high-impact sports or tight, pointy shoes until symptoms subside.

Medication & Injections• NSAIDs: may help reduce pain and swelling but often offer only temporary relief.• Corticosteroid injections: widely used to dampen inflammation; relief can last weeks to months but may weaken soft tissues with repeated use.• PRP for Morton's neuroma: an alternative to steroids with potentially longer-lasting results and fewer tissue-weakening effects. Physical & Alternative Therapies• Ultrasound-guided hydrodissection: fluid injection to separate the nerve from surrounding tissue.• Nerve gliding exercises: gentle mobilization techniques to reduce scar tethering.• Shockwave therapy or laser therapy: anecdotal reports suggest benefit; evidence is still emerging.

Neuromodulating Medications• Gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin) or certain antidepressants (amitriptyline) may help if neuropathic pain features dominate.• These require close monitoring for side effects like drowsiness or dizziness.

Advanced & Surgical Options• Cryoablation or radiofrequency ablation: targeted nerve freezing or heating to interrupt pain signals.• Neurectomy: surgical removal of the affected nerve segment can offer up to 85% long-term relief but carries risks of numbness, wound healing complications or recurrence.• Surgery is generally reserved for those who have tried—and not responded to—less invasive measures.

Setting Realistic Expectations

• No single treatment works for everyone.• Full recovery may take weeks to months, especially if nerve changes have been present for a long time.• Multimodal approaches—combining footwear changes, physical therapy, injections and lifestyle adjustments—tend to yield the best outcomes.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

Always consult a healthcare professional if you experience:– Severe, unrelenting foot pain that worsens at night– Signs of infection (redness, warmth, fever) after an injection– New numbness or muscle weakness in your foot– Any symptoms suggesting a life- or limb-threatening condition
