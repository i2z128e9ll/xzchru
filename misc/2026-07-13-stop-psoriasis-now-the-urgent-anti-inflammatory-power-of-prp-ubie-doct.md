Explanation

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin condition affecting about 2–3% of the population. It often appears as red, scaly patches on the skin and scalp. While there's no cure, treatments aim to reduce inflammation, slow skin cell growth, and relieve itching. In recent years, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has emerged as a potential add-on therapy. Below, we explore the science, benefits, risks, and real-world evidence behind PRP for psoriasis.

What Is PRP and How Is It Made?

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is derived from your own blood. A simple overview of the process:

A sample of your blood is drawn.

It's spun in a centrifuge to concentrate platelets into a small volume of plasma.

The resulting plasma contains 3–7× more platelets than whole blood, plus a mix of growth factors and cytokines.

This "platelet soup" is injected or microneedled into affected skin or scalp areas.

Because it's autologous (from you), there's minimal risk of allergic reaction or rejection.

How PRP Works: Anti-Inflammatory and Healing Actions

PRP's potential benefits stem from its rich content of growth factors and signaling proteins:

VEGF (Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor): Encourages new blood vessel formation.

PDGF (Platelet-Derived Growth Factor): Promotes cell growth and tissue repair.

TGF-β (Transforming Growth Factor beta): Modulates inflammation and supports collagen production.

IL-1ra (Interleukin-1 Receptor Antagonist): Counteracts pro-inflammatory IL-1.

In psoriasis—driven by overactive immune signals like TNF-α and IL-17—PRP can help rebalance local inflammation, accelerate skin healing, and reduce itch and scaling.

Evidence for PRP in Skin Psoriasis

While large-scale trials are still pending, early studies show promise:

A 2021 pilot study (n=20) reported a 30–50% reduction in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores after three monthly PRP sessions, with minimal side effects.

Patients noted smoother skin texture, thinner plaques, and less flaking.

No serious adverse events; mild bruising or soreness at injection sites was transient.

Evidence for PRP in Scalp Psoriasis

Scalp psoriasis can be especially stubborn because hair interferes with topical treatments. PRP offers a more direct approach:

A small trial (n=15) found significant decreases in redness, thickness, and scaling after two PRP sessions spaced one month apart.

Patients reported relief from itching and flaking within 2–3 weeks.

PRP was injected at multiple points across the scalp; pain was mild and brief.

What to Expect During a PRP Session

Consultation: Review your medical history, current medications, and psoriasis severity.

Blood Draw: A small sample (10–20 mL) is taken from your arm.

Centrifugation: The lab spins the sample for 10–15 minutes.

Injection or Microneedling: PRP is carefully injected into plaques or applied via microneedling pens.

Post-Care: Mild redness or tenderness can last 1–2 days; avoid harsh topicals for 24–48 hours.

Typical treatment plans involve 2–4 sessions spaced 3–6 weeks apart. Maintenance sessions every 3–6 months may help sustain results.

Next Steps: Talking to Your Doctor

PRP for psoriasis is an evolving area of dermatology. Before pursuing this treatment:

Discuss your full medical history with a board-certified dermatologist or rheumatologist.

Review alternative and standard therapies (topicals, phototherapy, biologics).

Ask about the provider's PRP preparation method and safety protocols.

Be clear on expected outcomes, costs, and follow-up plans.

Always speak to a doctor if you experience severe symptoms—such as widespread rash, intense pain, fever, or signs of infection—since these could signal life-threatening complications.

PRP offers a promising, low-risk approach to managing scalp and skin psoriasis by harnessing your body's own healing signals.
