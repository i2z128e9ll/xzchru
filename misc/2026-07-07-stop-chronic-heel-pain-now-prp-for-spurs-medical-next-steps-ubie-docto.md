Common causes of chronic heel pain:

Plantar fasciitis (inflammation of the tissue along the bottom of the foot)

Heel spurs (bony growths on the heel bone)

Achilles tendonitis or bursitis

Nerve entrapment or stress fractures

PRP for Heel Spurs and Medically Approved Next Steps

Persistent heel pain can be frustrating. If you've tried rest, stretching, and over-the-counter pain relievers without relief, you may have a heel spur or related condition. In this guide, we'll explain why your heel might still be aching, explore Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) therapy for heel spurs, and outline medically approved next steps.

Why Heel Pain Persists

Heel pain is often more complex than it seems. Common contributors include:

Plantar fasciitis: inflammation of the thick band of tissue (plantar fascia) under the foot.

Heel spurs: small bony growths on the underside of the heel bone, often developing with chronic plantar fasciitis.

Achilles tendinopathy: degeneration or inflammation of the Achilles tendon at the back of the heel.

Bursitis: irritation of the fluid-filled sac (bursa) that cushions the heel.

Nerve entrapment: compression of nerves near the heel causing burning or tingling.

Why pain lingers:

Micro-tears in soft tissues that don't heal fully.

Chronic inflammation that becomes self-sustaining.

Continued stress on the heel due to improper footwear, walking mechanics, or body weight.

When to Consider Advanced Therapies

If you've diligently followed first-line treatments for 6–12 weeks with little improvement, discuss advanced options with your healthcare provider:

Corticosteroid Injections– Fast relief by reducing local inflammation.– Risks include tissue weakening or tendon rupture if overused.

Extracorporeal Shock Wave Therapy (ESWT)– Sound waves stimulate healing in the plantar fascia.– Requires multiple sessions; mild discomfort during treatment.

PRP for Heel Spurs– Uses your own blood platelets to jump-start tissue repair.– Minimal risk of allergic reaction or rejection.

PRP for Heel Spurs: What You Need to Know

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) therapy has gained popularity for chronic tendon and soft-tissue injuries.

What Is PRP?

A blood draw concentrates platelets, which are rich in growth factors.

The PRP solution is injected into the injured area to promote healing.

How It Works

Growth factors in platelets stimulate cell recruitment and tissue regeneration.

May reduce chronic inflammation at the site of a heel spur or plantar fascia tear.

The PRP Procedure

Blood is drawn from your arm (similar to routine lab work).

A centrifuge spins the blood to separate platelets from red/white cells.

The provider injects the concentrated PRP into and around the heel spur under ultrasound guidance.

Treatment Schedule

Typically 1–3 injections spaced 2–4 weeks apart.

Total course depends on your response and severity of tissue damage.

Benefits and Expectations

Many patients report decreased pain and improved function in 6–12 weeks.

Unlike corticosteroids, PRP aims to heal rather than just mask symptoms.

Minimal downtime; resume light activity within days.

Potential Downsides

Not always covered by insurance.

Variable response—some patients experience significant relief, others less so.

Mild soreness at the injection site for a few days.

Evidence Summary:

Clinical studies show PRP can outperform corticosteroids in long-term pain relief for plantar fasciitis.

PRP's role specifically for heel spurs is promising but still evolving; discuss realistic expectations with your doctor.

Medically Approved Next Steps

If PRP isn't right for you or you want complementary strategies, consider:

Diagnostic Imaging

X-rays confirm heel spurs.

Ultrasound or MRI evaluates soft-tissue damage.

Physical Therapy

Targeted manual techniques, taping, and guided exercises.

Gait analysis to correct walking patterns.

Night Splints

Keep the ankle at 90° overnight to stretch the plantar fascia.

Can ease morning "first step" pain.

Weight Management

Even small weight loss reduces stress on the heel.

Balanced diet and low-impact exercise support overall recovery.

Surgical Consultation (rarely needed)

For persistent, disabling pain after 6–12 months of conservative and minimally invasive treatments.

Procedures may remove heel spurs or release part of the plantar fascia.

What You Can Do Right Now

Continue or restart conservative measures if you've stopped.

Track your pain levels, activities, and relief strategies in a daily journal.

Strengthen your calves and arches with the simple stretches described earlier.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

Heel pain rarely signals a life-threatening condition, but contact a healthcare professional if you experience:

Sudden, severe swelling or redness in the heel or ankle.

Signs of infection: fever, warmth, or pus around the injection or any wound.

Numbness, tingling, or foot drop.

Uncontrolled pain despite rest and pain relievers.

For any concerns that could be serious or life-threatening, always speak to a doctor right away.

Final Thoughts

Chronic heel pain from plantar fasciitis and heel spurs can change how you move and live. While initial treatments focus on rest, ice, stretching, and proper footwear, PRP for heel spurs offers a promising option for promoting real tissue healing. Work closely with your healthcare provider to tailor a plan that fits your goals and lifestyle. With patience, consistency, and the right medical guidance, you can get back on your feet—without the ache.
