# Sharpen Your Edge

From my seat on the plane, headrests seem to sprout hair. I count two comb-overs, a collection of mops in the brown-gray scale, and one spiky do, a waxy nest of hair horns. This is how the barber must see the world: as a jumble of potential haircuts and shaves, our heads and faces nothing more than his canvas, a living array of raw material.How the barber has fallen. His leatherette chair is largely empty these days; he's being done in by cheap chains, unisex salons, and the 17-bladed razor. The barber's pole, once a symbol of men's fashion, society, and health (barbers were the first surgeons, the striped pole representing the clean and bloodied bandages used in bloodletting), now seems like a nostalgic relic. Or worse, it seems a beacon for the cheap and the old, a place for the discerning man to avoid. In 1962, there were 180,000 barbershops in America. Now there are about 100,000, even though the U. S. male population has grown by more than 50 percent.Suburban kids like me, when it was time for a trim, accompanied our mothers to pastel-colored salons, put up with the acrid smells, and flipped though issues of Us Weekly while we waited for gum-chewing stylists named Jenny and Donna to tell us about their boyfriends while they worked. But the barbershop was a rare locale, the kind of place that helped ground us in our maleness without violence or competition. This face, this beard, this community (and occasionally, this dirty joke). I'm on a mission to rediscover the barber, and to find the enduring dignity of those masculine sanctuaries that still insist on hair-care establishments being like bathrooms: one for the ladies, one for the gentlemen.And so, London. After all, we brought our shaving bowls along with our buckled shoes when we set out from Mother England. My first stop was to be the Barber-Surgeons' Hall, home to the Worshipful Company of Barbers, one of the oldest guilds in London. But then came the brush-off : Their members are surgeons now, you see, and have no association with the "lowly trade of barbery." (That's a quote.) "I'm afraid we just can't help you," the spokesman demurred. "And I'd appreciate it if you didn't use my name."Whatever. The real history of the barber isn't in a clubhouse anyway. It's open for business, in places like the grand district of Mayfair. There, among palatial hotels and bespoke tailors, stand the so-called three T's of English barbering: Geo. F. Trumper, Taylor of Old Bond Street, and Truefitt and Hill. These shops have pruned privileged pates for a combined 500 years. The oldest, Truefitt and Hill, is offering me an apprenticeship (and an appointment). But if I'm to learn anything about the barber, I'll need a host of experiences, so I plan to make a few stops on the way there.A Haircutter Should Never Take ShortcutsOn the first floor of the stately Dorchester Hotel, past the doorman and the maid who looks straight from a fetish casting call, George and Lorenzo sit with the newspaper. The shop is empty. George rises to greet me, as if he's been awaiting my arrival. He's a youthful 63, with round spectacles and an easy smile. "We are original barbers, from Cyprus," he beams, arms wide. "Experts. There aren't many left." Lorenzo nods.I settle into a barber's chair while the duo holds court. What's wrong with the haircut chains, I ask. "Lady barbers cannot cut the man's hair," George grimaces, driving an imaginary trimmer through the air like a child would a race car. "They use the—the clippers."I quiz them about my current cut. "Lady barber," Lorenzo sniffs. "I see the line here." He places a thick finger behind his ear. Damn if he's not right. Lorenzo gathers his tools, and without asking, starts repairing the damage that's been done to my neckline.These men, clearly, are fighting for relevance. I can't agree with their sweeping sexism, but I hold to the warning: We must guard against laziness. In most shops and salons, mediocrity is the only thing on the menu. Stylists in chop shops—men and women—survive on volume, not craftsmanship. Speed has trumped skill. And most of us are so inured to it that we don't know the difference. The enemy isn't "lady barbers" or clippers, but complacency. In the barber's chair, and behind it.George and Lorenzo have it easy, though; they're barbers in a five-star hotel. They may not be busy, but they can charge $36 for a basic cut. What about the little guy, I wonder. What's he have to say about all of this?I'm about to find out.Haircutters are Not All the Same. Choose Yours WiselyThe best barbershops are word-of- mouth operations, and so to find a regular chap's barber, I need to track down a regular chap. Taxi!Paul is 47, and—from the rearview mirror of his cab, anyway—looks like Daniel Craig with watery eyes. "Spit and sawdust, is it?" he asks. "Sweeney Todd like?" He gives a mournful cluck. "Don't really exist anymore, fella." But he promises to take me to a barber who's good with a blade—and who charges only a few quid. "Turkish chap," Paul says, over the rumble of the cab. "Does your ears, too."My ears?In Holborn, Paul pulls up to a place called the Cutting Room. I step into a busy, modern shop, pulsing with dance music, and meet Mus (short for Mustafa). He's so soft-spoken I can hardly hear him over the music. "Turkish barbers are the best. We do the flame thing," he says, waggling his fingers at his ears. "No one does that."I'm saving my stubble for Truefitt and Hill, but nobody has dibs on my ears, so I grab a seat. Mus takes a lighter to something resembling an incense stick. An oily flame springs from the tip. Mus passes the flame over my right ear, using his free hand to fan it from my flesh before anything barbecues. A pleasant warmth tickles my lobe. Soon, the air is sharp with an unfamiliar, foul smell. "That's my ear hair!" I exclaim. "I'm smelling my own ear hair!" I pop up from the chair and turn to the men in the waiting area. "You guys do this? You get your ear hair burned off ?""That's why we come here, mate," one says.Apparently, I've been too casual with my definitions. Barbers are not all general practitioners; there are specialists for above-the-collar maintenance. Just as a dermatologist can't set a broken leg, a swanky salon can't singe unwanted hair from your ears or nose. A man should choose a specialist based on his own unique head-fur challenges. And if you're in London, and your ears are woolly, Mus is your go-to guy.Outside, I realize I should have asked him to do my other ear. With one still fuzzy and one burned clean, sensitive to the slightest breeze, I list starboard, like a cat on sedatives.Your Haircut Changes with the Times. So Should Its CaretakerThe Turkish barbers are wizards, but I'm curious about how other traditions have evolved. Murdock, a vintage shop in Shoreditch, looks the way I want to feel after a trip to a barber: masculine, sophisticated, cool. Owner Brendan Murdock has managed to blend retro hip with spalike product knowledge and selection.As I page through a men's magazine, circa 1900, filled with fusty fashions and style tips, I realize I've operated under an unconscious myth: that men didn't worry about their looks back when railroads were young and silicon was just sand."Men have always looked out for themselves," Murdock tells me. "The renewed interest in the old barbershops...is men just rediscovering something from their fathers' time, from their grandfathers' time." Of course, sons must reject their father's ideals and traditions—and trumpet their own—before adopting them. In East London, down narrow Brick Lane, amid the hurly-burly of Bengali merchants, Azeem Gents Hair Styles packs them in. A 21-year-old kid named H, fourth in line, waits outside with a cigarette. Inside, a grim barber named Salim uses a blade to carve crop circles into the back of a dude's head. The razor peels the black hair back like the skin of a fruit, revealing the pale meat of the skull beneath.Murdock and Azeem are at opposing ends of a long barbershop continuum, representing two extreme ways today's barber might define his trade. One is a willful attempt to arrest the clock and wrench it back. The other hurtles pell-mell toward a future in which we—perhaps—will draw constellations on our melons.And it's unfair to assume "classic" barbers lay greater claim on authenticity, or good taste, just because they evoke the romance of history. Some of the styles in Murdock's magazine (the chin curtain, anyone?) look as loony as a Charlie Brown stripe across your cranium.A man redefines himself every time he asks his barber for a new look—or the usual. More important than tethering yourself to a trend is finding a barber who elevates you, stimulates your sense of belonging, and reaffirms your best vision of yourself, be he flash with cornrows or flush with Italian shaving soap.A Haircut Builds a BondStill, it's impossible not to be seduced by history on Jermyn Street, the storied center of men's fashion. At Piccadilly Arcade, I eyeball a statue of Beau Brummell, the founder of dandyism, and will myself to emulate the man who made appearance a personal religion. (It's a little difficult, because he looks so dumb in the cravat.) Finally, I turn left onto St. James and see the sign for the oldest barber in the world: Truefitt and Hill, Est. 1805, Gentlemen's Hairdresser and Perfumers.I peer through the window at the rows of products assembled like some shrine to the shave. It's early yet, and the place is empty. Shop manager Dennis Hornsby, a big man stooped from 44 years behind a barber's chair, lets me in. A moment later, he shoos me out. The first customer of the morning is a former British prime minister. (I'm asked not to reveal his name.) They don't want a writer around—not even one apprenticing as a barber.Truefitt and Hill has been accustomed to high rollers ever since William Francis Truefitt was named Royal Wigmaker to King George III in the early 1800s. When Prince Philip, the Duke of Edinburgh, wants a trim, a car comes and takes Hornsby to Windsor Castle. Really, the only thing Truefitt and Hill has in common with the shop I go to in New York City is this: Both use scissors.After the former prime minister departs, Matthew, my master for the day, shows me how to prepare hot towels. I soak them in hot water, drain the excess, sprinkle them with West Indian Limes formula, roll them, and stash them in a contraption that looks like a toaster oven. I take coats, sweep up for Hornsby, and keep my head down. At one point I realize I'm standing next to a leather chair dated 1802. Most of the arrivals are rather thin-lidded. I wonder at the wisdom of dropping $51 to shorten such spartan pates. But they're here for the ritual. This is their barbershop. Where else would they go?Matthew teaches me to prepare shaving lather. The trick is to leave just the right amount of hot water in the tin mug to mix with the shaving cream. My first lather runs down a customer's neck like milky water. At Matthew's direction, I remake it, working the brush around furiously in the mug. This time, Matthew steps away. "Lather him up," he says.I consider the reclined stranger, swaddled in a blue smock. He looks like a sleeping child. He has put his good looks—and his jugular—in the hands of his barber, who, at this moment, is a journalist from the United States who has just whipped up the very first lather of his life.I stand above him, my first customer, and taste the power of that responsibility and the pride that follows it. I roll it around on my tongue. Then I put the brush to his cheek with my lather-smeared fingers, and instantly lose my grip. The brush bounces off his chest, spitting lather, and clatters to the floor. I shrink away from the customer, red-faced. Matthew rescues me, and I slink back to my broom. If these were the old days, I'd be getting an ass whipping right now for my trespasses.A little later, when I take my turn in the barber's chair, the stress of my fumble fades. Charles Dickens, Oscar Wilde, and Sir Winston Churchill all called Truefitt and Hill's home. John Wayne and Frank Sinatra were frequent visitors. And I'd joined this fraternity of men just by having a seat.Truefitt and Hill's almond shaving cream is luxurious. The warm nudge of the badger-hair brush is oddly meditative, and the hot towel sublime. Heat subsumes my face. It's hard to imagine I deserve such pampering. Matthew's razor progresses across my skin in short, smooth stokes, changing direction as my whiskers dictate, never going against the grain. He speaks to me in hushed tones. We're connected now. Partners. It is a terribly intimate thing to allow someone such unrestricted access to your face. As the barber, I felt (for a moment) the rush of receiving a customer's trust. Now, as the customer, I feel the unique pleasure of giving it. The bond is palpable.After a spritzing of aftershave, I open my eyes to the wooden plaque on the wall above my chair, proclaiming Truefitt and Hill's Royal Warrant to the Duke of Edinburgh. I hear the low murmur of conversation behind me, the click of Hornsby's scissors. I reconsider an old phrase, one I'd never fully appreciated until now: It's good to be king.Duke, too, turns out. Duke ain't bad either.The London sky makes good on a 2-day threat. Rain falls wearily, as though the droplets can't muster enthusiasm for the job. I gather my things as Hornsby sweeps the day's last trimmings. The locks, cut and swept away, will soon regrow, only to be cut again by Hornsby, or someone like him.The cycle continues, not so different now from what it was in 1805 when razors drew blood and hair appeased the gods. Perhaps we shouldn't be so quick to put "barber" on the endangered species list. No, suddenly one thing is clear: The barber's place is secure for as long as man grows hair on his head.I step out, no umbrella, into the rain. I've been to my barber, bud. I'm ready.
