# Giancarlo Stanton Hasn't Been the Same Since His Gruesome Injury. Somehow, He's Better

Giancarlo Stanton was down in the count: one ball, two strikes. He was just trying to make solid contact, he later explained, which is why his home run off Pirates pitcher Tyler Glasnow last June traveled a mere 449 feet, considerably shorter than many moonshots Stanton has launched in his pro career, dating back to his time in the minors. (We're thinking, in particular, of the 500-plus-foot meteorite he sent screaming over the scoreboard of the Double-A Montgomery Biscuits in 2010.)

Stanton's clout off Glasnow, though modest by his own standards, came within inches of exiting Pittsburgh's PNC Park and finding the Allegheny River. "Do you ever take that for granted," a reporter asked Stanton after the game, "that you can hit a ball that far?"

"No, never take it for granted," replied Stanton, then in his eighth season with the Miami Marlins. If you do that, he added, "this game will hit you right in the mouth." He wasn't speaking figuratively.

Stanton was leading the National League in home runs (37) on September 11, 2014, when he stepped in against the Milwaukee Brewers' Mike Fiers. It would be his last at bat of the season. With the count 0-1, Fiers delivered an 88 mph fastball that got away from him. The ball stoved in the left side of Stanton's face, fracturing bones, knocking out teeth, damaging his jaw, and creating a laceration that required more than 20 stitches to close. After medical personnel attended to him for several minutes, Stanton was placed on a gurney and wheeled off the field. Distraught after watching Stanton crumple to the ground, Fiers proceeded to plunk the next batter too. Players from both teams poured onto the field.

It boded well for Stanton's comeback that, as he saw the benches empty — he was watching on a small TV in the bowels of Miller Park — he tried to rise from his gurney and join the fray. "I wanted to get out there and get mine in," he recalls, laughing, "but I was strapped in."

To say that Stanton, 28, has made a complete recovery is a glaring understatement. He led the majors last season with 59 home runs. Only one other big leaguer broke the 50-homer barrier in 2017: Aaron Judge. And at 6' 7" and 282 pounds, Judge has an inch and almost 40 pounds on him.

Judge, of course, clubbed 52 homers in his rookie season with the Yankees. When reports started emerging that New York was trading for Stanton, Judge tweeted, "Hey @Giancarlo818 [fist-bump emoji]. . ." above a GIF of Will Ferrell asking John C. Reilly in the movie Step Brothers, "Did we just become best friends?"

Two days later, rumor became fact. The redwood-sized power hitters had joined forces, leaving many Yankees fans to wonder just who, exactly, was Judge's new BBF. (That would be Bash Bro Forever.)

Scouts first posed that question when Stanton was a teenager, back when he went by Mike. (His full name is Giancarlo Cruz-Michael Stanton.) A three-sport athlete at Notre Dame High, a private school in Sherman Oaks, California, Stanton was a stud wide receiver and cornerback coveted by then-USC coach Pete Carroll. He was also a Rodman-esque rebounder averaging 13 points per game for the basketball team. But in baseball? He was only a middling outfielder. The sport that eventually landed him a 13-year, $325 million contract — the most lucrative deal in the history of North American sports — was actually his third best when he was growing up.

In addition to averaging about 26 yards per catch as a receiver, Stanton was one of California's best punters. But he didn't punt until his senior year. Before that, all kicks were handled by Kai Forbath, now with the Vikings.

"I never questioned if I was going to get back to where I was. Just maybe how long it would take."

As a junior, Stanton was overshadowed on the diamond by lots of other Southern California schoolboys. "He just needed to play more," says Tom Dill, his skipper at Notre Dame. "Out here, kids play baseball year-round — it gives them a big advantage — and he wasn't."

Stanton's breakout moment, by all accounts, came during the summer before his senior year. That August, he was a no-namer competing alongside future first-round picks Mike Moustakas and Aaron Hicks at Long Beach's Area Code Baseball Games, a showcase of young talent. Stanton took about 20 swings in batting practice and hit a handful of balls out of the park, including two that went rustling through treetops some 40 feet above the fence. Two balls even sailed over the access road beyond the stadium and into a nearby golf course. After that jaw-dropping display of power, a throng of scouts made their way over to the rangy slugger, some of them going so far as to ask, "Who are you?"

American League pitchers are about to find out for themselves, especially those in the AL East, who will face the Bronx Bombers 19 times and must now reckon with a murderers' row featuring the dueling yetis Judge and Stanton, plus catcher Gary Sanchez (33 homers in '17) and shortstop Didi Gregorius (25). When asked by a reporter how difficult life will be for opposing pitchers, Stanton replied, drily, "I feel sorry for the baseballs too."

It is a league without pity, as Stanton was reminded upon returning to the lineup in 2015, a custom face guard bolted to the left side of his helmet.

He told Men's Health, "For two years, pitchers would throw up and in to scare me, just to get me thinking about that." They were probing, testing, spelunking in his head, trying to trigger him, to stir up some post-traumatic stress. "I was aware of it," he says. "But in terms of messing with me as a hitter, it never did."

If the addition of Stanton to their roster is enough to nudge these youthful Yankees into the World Series (they fell one win short last season), it will be due in large part to his ability to work his way out of a dark place. Plenty of big leaguers have taken a pitch above the neck. Not all are the same when they return. If they ever return at all.

In his 2013 book, The Sports Gene, David Epstein explains that about half that time is chewed up as the information is received by the retinas (a baseball has been sighted and is inbound!) and then conveyed across synapses in the visual cortex. "The window for actually making contact with the ball, when it is in reach of the bat, is 5 milliseconds," Epstein writes, "and because the angular position of the ball relative to the hitter's eye changes so rapidly as it gets closer to the plate," it's literally impossible to obey the earnest counsel given by every Little League father in history: Keep your eye on the ball! Then how does Stanton even make contact, let alone crush those 59 homers last season that traveled a total of 4.67 miles? He does so by collecting a set of cues to essentially predict the future. This is what prominent Carnegie Mellon University psychologists William G. Chase and Herbert A. Simon referred to as the "chunking theory" of expertise. As Epstein explains further in his book, "Rather than grappling with a large number of individual pieces, experts unconsciously group information into a smaller number of meaningful chunks based on patterns that they have seen before." In other words, the late great baseball philosopher Yogi Berra had it right when he said, "You can't think and hit at the same time."

Professional hitters step into the box and soft-focus on the upper half of a pitcher's body, eventually zeroing in on the release point. Subconsciously they're looking for recognizable tells or patterns. If you can see more of the pitcher's hand, it's a changeup. If his hand is on the side of the ball, it's a slider. If the seams form a red dot, it's a curveball. Even a pitcher's arm angle can help you determine where the ball will end up — and it's all vital information, because you have to decide whether to swing or not before the ball is about halfway to the plate.

But chunking can't happen when the brain finds no similar previous experiences, no helpful precedents.
