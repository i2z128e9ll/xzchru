2. Infection: Bacterial, Viral, and Sexually Transmitted

Infectious causes of rectal pain often present with additional signs such as discharge, fever, or systemic symptoms. Common pathogens include gonorrhea, chlamydia, herpes simplex, syphilis, and enteric bacteria.

Deep, throbbing or crampy rectal pain

Anal or rectal discharge (pus, mucus, or blood-tinged)

Painful tenesmus (urgent, incomplete sensation of needing to pass stool)

Fever, chills, or generalized malaise

Genital sores, vesicles, or ulcers (with herpes or syphilis)

Risk Factors & History

Recent unprotected anal intercourse

Multiple sexual partners or known exposure to an infected partner

Recent antibiotic use (disrupting normal gut flora)

Travel history (e.g., areas with poor sanitation)

Diagnostic Clues

Anoscopy or proctoscopy may reveal mucosal ulcers, friability, or exudate

Swabs for nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) for gonorrhea and chlamydia

Culture or PCR for herpes simplex virus

Stool studies if bacterial enteritis is suspected

Treatment Principles (based on 2015 STD Guidelines)

Chlamydia: Doxycycline or azithromycin alone

3. Inflammatory Disease: Ulcerative Colitis, Crohn's Disease, Cirrhosis-Related Changes

Chronic inflammatory conditions can involve the rectum directly or lead to secondary vascular changes due to portal hypertension in cirrhosis.

Key Symptoms of Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

Persistent or recurrent rectal pain lasting weeks to months

Frequent loose stools mixed with blood and mucus

Urgent bowel movements, sometimes at night

Systemic signs: weight loss, fatigue, low-grade fever

Extraintestinal manifestations: joint pain, skin rashes, eye inflammation

Diagnostic Features

Colonoscopy with biopsies showing mucosal ulceration, crypt abscesses (ulcerative colitis), or transmural inflammation and granulomas (Crohn's)

Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR)

Fecal calprotectin to indicate intestinal inflammation

Cirrhosis and Portal Hypertension

Advanced liver disease can lead to portal hypertension, causing rectal varices that may bleed or become painful. Although less common than hemorrhoids, variceal bleeding is serious.

Signs may include:

Painless bleeding initially, but large clots can cause distension pain

Signs of chronic liver disease: jaundice, spider angiomas, ascites, leg edema

Laboratory evidence: low platelets, prolonged clotting times, elevated liver enzymes

Management focuses on treating the underlying liver disease (e.g., antiviral therapy, alcohol cessation) and controlling variceal bleeding with endoscopic band ligation or vasoactive drugs.

4. Putting It All Together: A Symptom Checklist

Systemic Signs: Fever, chills, night sweats, weight loss?

Associated Lesions: Lumps, ulcers, vesicles, skin tags?

Rectal pain can stem from simple irritation, infection, or chronic inflammatory disease.
