# New Research Offers Hope in the Fight Against Alzheimer’s

This piece is part of “The Cure for Everything:” A series of stories that looks at the breakthroughs, setbacks, and overall status of vaccines and cures for hard-to-treat diseases and viruses, from HIV and herpes to the flu and hangovers.

YOU CAN FIGHT heart disease, cancer, and strokes with an arsenal of medications, surgeries, and other tech. You can fortify your defenses against Covid with a vaccine. But Alzheimer’s? The weapons against it are few and weak.

Only two medications are believed to slow down Alzheimer’s Disease, and a third was recently pulled from the market in a whirlwind of controversy over how it was approved and marketed. In case you're wondering if Alzheimer's is the same as dementia, it's not, but they are related. Dementia is an umbrella term that describes a wide range of symptoms, including declines in memory, judgement, reasoning, and focus, as well as changes in language and behavior. Dementia causes disabilities in daily function, like managing medications, finances, appointments, cooking, cleaning, and driving. Alzheimer's is a specific brain disease marked by symptoms of dementia that gradually get worse over time. Alzheimer's disease first affects the part of the brain associated with learning, so early symptoms often include changes in memory, thinking, and reasoning skills. Alzheimer's can also cause mild cognitive impairment, a stage prior to dementia, when people can still be capable and independent.

While there's no cure yet for Alzheimer's, researchers are finally making more progress in better understanding the disease and working on new treatments that may even reverse it.

When Toxic Proteins Tangle Your Brain

Scientists have been trying to pinpoint the cause of Alzheimer’s Disease since psychiatrist Alois Alzheimer, M.D. first described it in 1906. When Dr. Alzheimer autopsied a woman with unusual memory loss, he saw plaques and nerve tangles in her brain. Since then, brain scans and other tech have revealed more clues to how it starts.

“There are some proteins in the brain that actually start to dysfunction, become toxic, and they come together and get deposited in the brain,” says Suman De, Ph.D., a research fellow at the University of Sheffield’s Institute for Translational Neuroscience.

These proteins are beta-amyloid plaques, which accumulates around your brain’s nerve cells, or neurons, and tau proteins, which tangle inside the cells. Beta-amyloid and tau accumulate for decades before symptoms appear, damaging neurons, shrinking your brain, and eventually causing organ failure. But why does the whole process start? That’s still a mystery.

“We don't fully understand what is causing Alzheimer's disease,” says Nathaniel Chin, M.D., an associate professor at the University of Wisconsin and medical director of the Wisconsin Alzheimer's Disease Research Center. “We know that these proteins are building up, this protein of amyloid, this protein of tau, but we don't yet fully grasp what is driving the buildup.”

Genetic factors, inflammation, insulin resistance, and toxin exposure might encourage beta-amyloid and tau to go rogue and build up faster, triggering Alzheimer’s Disease. But there’s also debate about whether these proteins are driving the disease at all. Some scientists, including teams from Columbia University and the Universities of Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, and the Sorbonne say beta-amyloid and tau might build up in response to Alzheimer’s Disease rather than causing it.

Even if scientists can't agree on a cause for Alzheimer’s, there’s the problem of when to start looking for it. Any cure would need to prevent or unwind decades of damage that happen before symptoms like memory loss appear. Say a person started developing Alzheimer’s in their 40s. “When we look at the brain with imaging, we find these big deposits when they are 70, but by that time 20 percent, 30 percent of brain tissue, or even more, is actually lost,” says De.

Once you have lost brain tissue, you’re running a race you can’t win. “We can actually build more brain cells and more connections among brain cells, but never at the rate at which we lose them once a person has dementia,” says Dr. Chin.

Until recently, symptoms were the only clue that Alzheimer’s had arrived. “It's only in the past 10 to 15 years that the technology has gotten us to a point where we can reliably and accurately detect the proteins of Alzheimer's, amyloid and tau, in people with symptoms, and now without symptoms,” he says. First came lumbar punctures, where a small needle is inserted into your back to collect spinal fluid to be tested for the proteins. Then came positron emission tomography (PET) scans, which light up the proteins with radioactive tracers. Now, blood tests can detect the proteins and are increasingly used in people with mild cognitive impairment. A new study from Sweden suggests blood tests are 90 percent accurate in predicting Alzheimer’s.

These tech advancements are opening a window of opportunity to catch changes early and start treatment, but there’s one more problem. It’s tough to figure out where Alzheimer’s ends and other age-related brain problems, like strokes and Lewy body dementia, begin. “There are probably multiple factors happening when a person is experiencing thinking change,” says Dr. Chin. “And what we found is even treating one disease like Alzheimer's, there are probably other things happening in the brain that we have to identify and treat as well.”

The Quest for the Next Breakthrough

Since Alzheimer’s was first linked to beta-amyloid and tau in the 1980s, drugmakers have largely focused on thwarting these two proteins. More than 40 medications targeting beta-amyloid or tau have been developed only to fail in clinical trials. One of the biggest setbacks came in 2002, when a clinical trial for AN1792, a potential Alzheimer’s vaccine, was stopped early. The vaccine cleared plaques from participants’ brains but had a scary side effect–6 percent developed dangerous brain inflammation.

Until 2021, all Alzheimer’s drugs just treated symptoms–none worked on the causes of the disease itself. But the FDA has recently approved three monoclonal antibody medications thought to slow down Alzheimer’s Disease by stimulating the immune system to clear out harmful brain plaques.

The first new drug to treat Alzheimer’s in 17 years, Aduhelm (aducanumab), came out in 2021, but it’s already off the market. The research behind Aduhelm showed mixed results, and the medication carried a hefty price tag of $56,000 per month. Three FDA advisors resigned over the decision to approve Aduhelm, with one saying it was “probably the worst drug approval decision in recent U.S. history.” Then the drug was linked to bleeding and swelling in the brain, symptoms not unlike those found in patients in the early aughts vaccine trial.

Leqembi (lecanemab), which slows cognitive decline by 27 percent over 18 months, came out in 2023. Controversy has followed this drug, too, because it can also cause brain swelling and bleeding. “At an individual level, we all have to choose what we're comfortable with, what level of risk we are willing to take for this potential benefit,” says Dr. Chin.

Kisunla (donanemab-azbt), which slows cognitive and functional decline by up to 35 percent over 18 months, came out in 2024. Patients only need a monthly dose and can stop once plaques are cleared. “From a scientific biological perspective, it is pretty incredible in what it can do,” says Dr. Chin. “It removes huge amounts of amyloid protein in the brain.”

Like Leqembi, Kisunla can lead to brain swelling and bleeding, so there’s a tug of war between risks and benefits. Researchers are also studying these drugs for potential use as preventive treatments and combining them with other therapies, like focused ultrasound, in hopes of hastening plaque clearance.

Other treatment hopefuls are in earlier stages. There are 124 potential Alzheimer’s treatments now being tested in clinical trials to target beta-amyloid and tau, reduce inflammation, protect neurons, act on genes that increase Alzheimer’s risk, and more. “It's not just about amyloid and tau,” says Dr. Chin. “The actual clinical trial field is very diverse.”

And the quest to develop a vaccine continues, with clinical trial results expected in just a few years. Swedish biopharmaceutical company Alzinova, Swiss biopharmaceutical company AC Immune SA, and the California-based Institute for Molecular Medicine are studying vaccine options in people in the early stages of Alzheimer’s. The idea is that the shots could stimulate your immune system to clear harmful protein buildups in your brain.

Other researchers are exploring whether existing vaccines might help with Alzheimer’s, too. Harvard researchers are testing whether the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin vaccine, used to prevent tuberculosis, can reduce tau protein buildup in people at high risk of developing Alzheimer’s. Researchers in Wisconsin are studying whether the commonly used Tdap vaccine for tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis might reduce Alzheimer’s risk, based on the idea that pertussis bacteria might trigger the development of brain plaques in Alzheimer’s.

While no one has yet found a cure for Alzheimer’s “we are closer than ever before,” says De.

What You Can Do to Fight Alzheimer’s Today

Until there’s a cure for Alzheimer’s Disease, your best bet is to double-down on your overall best practices for brain health now—whatever your age. “Alzheimer's is actually a young person's problem because you have more control and more ability to preserve your brain and keep it the best possible for as long as possible,” says Dr. Chin. Plus, while most people are 65 or older when they’re diagnosed with Alzheimer’s, about 10 percent have early-onset disease. The youngest guy on record developed symptoms at just 17. Here’s how to stay sharp:

Move it or lose it.

“I ask my patients to exercise every day, even if it's 15 minutes versus 50 minutes, whatever they feel comfortable doing without hurting themselves,” says Dr. Chin. “I do think that that makes the biggest difference.” When you exercise, your brain releases brain-derived neurotrophic factor, or BDNF, which has been called Miracle-Gro for the brain because it helps generate new cells. Research from the National Institutes of Health suggests the neuro-protective effects of physical activity might be even bigger for people carrying APOE e4, a gene linked to increased Alzheimer’s risk.

Mind your diet.

Try a diet high in antioxidant-rich brain foods. Research published in Neurology showed that people who followed the Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) Diet, an eating plan rich in produce and low in salt and processed foods, had fewer Alzheimer's-related brain changes than those who didn’t. (People who ate the most leafy greens fared particularly well.)

Sleep well to stay sharp.

During deep sleep, your glymphatic system, or your brain’s sewage system, clears out harmful proteins before they cluster. Sleep problems like insomnia, snoring, nighttime waking, and sleep-related movement disorders are linked with a higher risk of developing Alzheimer’s, suggests a study review in the Journal of Neurology. If sleep problems plague you, talk to your doctor about seeing a sleep specialist.

Make new things.

“The same way you exercise your body every day, please exercise your brain,” says Dr. Chin. “Do something that's fun but challenging to you.” In a study published in the journal Psychological Research, people over 50 who regularly did things that were active, novel, and productive, like writing, had better memories of events and language than people who did things that were more passive, familiar, or receptive, like reading, solving crosswords, and watching TV.

Call your friends.

People with weak social networks are 67 percent more likely to develop Alzheimer’s than those with stronger connections, according to new research from the University of Hawaii. Socializing might boost your cognitive reserve, your brain’s stockpile of smarts that makes you more resilient against aging, the researchers say. Plus, your friends might help you stick with healthy habits that protect your brain.

Practice mindfulness.

A study from McGill University showed that people who live more mindfully–verbally labeling and expressing their experiences, paying conscious attention to the present moment, not judging their experiences, and allowing experiences to arise without reacting–have less cognitive decline and less harmful proteins in their brains than those who approach life less mindfully. “Mindfulness, walking out in nature, petting your dog–these are things that people are looking at as ways of lowering cortisol, lowering your sympathetic nervous system, so that ideally leads to better brain health,” says Dr. Chin.

Ask yourself the right questions.

If you aren’t as sharp as you used to be, Alzheimer’s is just one of many possible culprits. “The first thing a person needs to do is really reflect: What is it that I'm experiencing? What's the context in which I have these symptoms? Did I just lose a loved one? Am I incredibly stressed at work? Is my diabetes poorly controlled?” says Dr. Chin. Then, ask a friend if they’ve noticed so you have a solid second opinion. Share your notes with your doctor and say you want to be checked for thinking changes.
