Explanation

Hip pain can disrupt daily activities, athletic pursuits, and overall quality of life. One common culprit is gluteal tendinopathy—injury or degeneration of the tendons around the hip, especially the gluteus medius and minimus. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections have grown in popularity as a minimally invasive treatment. But is PRP for gluteal tendinopathy truly effective?

Understanding Gluteal Tendinopathy

Gluteal tendinopathy involves irritation or microtears in the tendons that attach the hip abductor muscles (gluteus medius and minimus) to the femur. Key points:

Symptoms

Dull, aching pain on the outside of the hip

Pain aggravated by walking, climbing stairs, lying on the affected side

Weakness when lifting the leg sideways

Risk factors

Age (more common over 40)

Overuse (running, standing for long periods)

Biomechanical issues (leg length discrepancy, muscle imbalances)

What Is PRP and How Might It Help?

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is derived from your own blood. It contains a high concentration of platelets, each loaded with growth factors that promote tissue repair. Here's how PRP is prepared and theorized to work:

Blood Draw• A small amount of your blood is collected.

Centrifugation• Blood is spun in a centrifuge to concentrate platelets.

Injection• The PRP solution is injected directly into the injured tendon under ultrasound guidance.

Proposed benefits:

Stimulates collagen production and tendon healing

Reduces chronic inflammation

May shorten recovery time compared to natural healing

What Does the Evidence Say?

Research into PRP for gluteal tendinopathy is growing, though still evolving. Key findings from recent studies include:

Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)

Some RCTs show PRP provides greater pain relief and functional improvement at 6–12 months compared to corticosteroid injections.

Other trials find no significant difference between PRP and placebo injections.

Observational Studies

Many report 60–80% of patients experience meaningful pain reduction and improved hip function after PRP.

Benefits often appear by 3 months and can last up to a year or more.

Systematic Reviews

Meta-analyses conclude PRP may be more effective than steroids in the long term, though more high-quality, standardized studies are needed.

Bottom line: PRP shows promise, especially for chronic cases unresponsive to conservative treatments. However, protocols (platelet concentration, number of injections) vary widely, making direct comparisons tricky.

Comparing PRP to Other Treatments

Before considering PRP, most clinicians recommend a stepwise approach:

Conservative Management

Rest and activity modification

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

Physical therapy focused on hip strengthening and stretching

Injection Therapies

Corticosteroid injections: Quick relief but potential tendon weakening with repeated use

PRP injections: Slower onset, aim to heal rather than suppress inflammation

Advanced Options

Shockwave therapy

Tenotomy or tendon repair surgery (in severe, refractory cases)

PRP often enters the picture after a minimum of 3–6 months of conservative care without significant improvement.

Potential Risks and Side Effects

PRP is generally safe, but no treatment is entirely risk-free. Possible complications include:

Temporary pain or soreness at the injection site

Infection (rare, less than 1%)

Nerve irritation if the needle contacts surrounding nerves

Practical Considerations

Before scheduling a PRP injection, consider:

Provider expertise: Seek a clinician experienced in musculoskeletal ultrasound–guided injections.

Preparation protocols: Ask about platelet concentration and whether leukocytes are included.

Number of injections: Many protocols recommend 1–3 injections spaced 4–6 weeks apart.

Cost and insurance coverage: Verify out-of-pocket expenses and potential reimbursement.

Rehab plan: A structured post-injection physical therapy program is essential to maximize outcomes.

When to Consider PRP for Gluteal Tendinopathy

You may be a candidate for PRP if:

You've tried at least 3–6 months of conservative care (rest, NSAIDs, physical therapy) with persistent pain.

Imaging (ultrasound or MRI) confirms tendon degeneration or tears.

You prefer a biological approach over corticosteroids or surgery.

You're informed about costs, risks, and the possibility that PRP may not fully resolve symptoms.
